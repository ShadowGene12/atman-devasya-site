# So Shall We

The clatter of plates and the bubbling of soapy water in the kitchen sink filled Abhimanyu’s ears. He was deeply invested in his kitchen duties, being the regular helping hand.

“Give it a rest, kid. Leave some of the work for tomorrow. That’s the third time you’ve washed that same plate… Kid, are you lost in some fairy tale world again?”

“Oh, sorry, Uncle Gupta. I was just thinking about my college thesis. In fact, I’m so close to finishing the prototype.”

“Look, don’t mind me, but I don’t understand all that science stuff, kid. But I hope you get out of this dishwashing and odd-job life and earn fair pay. A bright boy like you shouldn’t be stuck doing tasks meant for scrawny old men like us.”

“I don’t consider you scrawny, Uncle. And soon enough, I’ll be graduating college… so no more odd jobs after that, hopefully.”

“I’ll be glad to see it. Just make sure you remember your old pal.”

“Is that a request? What’s mine is yours, Uncle Gupta.”

“Hey, Brainiac…” A soft voice whispered as gentle hands covered Abhi’s eyes. For a moment, his tired expression melted into a smile, knowing exactly who would disrupt his work hours.

“For the nth time, Aadhya, stop… It’s annoying when you disturb me while I’m working.”

“Master Rathore, your work hours start first thing in the morning, answering questions for professors, then dedicating yourself to Professor Hans for your projects. And after all that… I expect the genius I love to spend a few moments with me. But instead, he wants to wash dishes and cook in a third-rate restaurant where barely even ghosts come to eat.”

“Ouch, offense taken!” Uncle Gupta chimed in with a grin.

“Sorry, Uncle Gupta, I love dropping truth bombs!”

“Aadhya, just because the manager isn’t around and only Uncle is here doesn’t mean you can just speak your mind like that,” Abhimanyu said. “Besides, he pays me for my work.”

“Pay? Please, dude! Now, I ain’t no princess, or a governor, or an Astral… but I’d rather get paid more somewhere else than this place you call work.”

“Are you here just to torment me?”

“Yes, totally. But more than that… I’m here to tell you: it’s your birthday, genius! Happy Birthday!”

She planted a warm kiss on Abhi’s cheek, making him blush.

“Sorry to disturb you,” she teased. “You’re so busy with everything you do that you forget the most important day of your life, dude.”

“Aadhya, I didn’t forget… It just wasn’t that important.”

“Well, it was to me. Me and Uncle Gupta made sure to decorate this place and surprise you.”

“Woah… I have no words. Thanks, my love.”

She took his hand and led him into the restaurant’s private back room. Hanging on the wall was a poster filled with childhood memorabilia and photos, every detail carefully chosen to celebrate him.

Touched, Abhimanyu smiled warmly. Aadhya knew her mission was accomplished.

“Now stop with the puppy face and cut the cake. I’m starving. And mind you, if I gain weight, I’m making you run laps around the university grounds with me!”

“Fine… I cannot argue with that.”

Abhi brought the knife down to cut the small cake she had brought—decorated with a sweet illustration of the two of them holding hands, looking out toward the vast ocean.

They celebrated, eating until their bellies were full.

“Oh boy, I’m so full right now,” Abhi groaned. “I need to clean up all your decorations before the manager spots them tomorrow.”

“It’s alright, kid,” Uncle Gupta said, waving him away. “Take the night off. It’s your day, I’ll take care of it.”

“I don’t want to cause you trouble, Uncle…”

“Then pay me back later! Just go now!”

***

The midnight air chilled their clothes, their coats wrapped tightly against the breeze. The moon shone at its brightest, accompanied by the songs of night owls and crickets.

The duo walked until they reached the park zone. Inside, the world was peacefully quiet—bushes whispering in the wind, fireflies drifting between the branches.

“So… is Mumma doing fine?”

“Yes, she is,” Abhi replied softly. “You know how it is. Two kids to look after, managing the finances of a struggling household. I’m just glad Didi got a great job offer. She was offered a transfer to the UK, but as always, she wouldn’t take it no matter the pay.”

“Abhi… it’s been five years. You are on the verge of graduating. Stop taking these low-paying jobs where people don’t treat you with the respect you deserve.”

“No job is low, Aadhya. I have to help Mom however I can.”

“She never wanted you to earn through means that wear you down. Besides, you’re the top-ranking student at the university! Professor Tavin Hans has already taken you on as his trainee. The entire scientific community is going to pursue you… and yet you spend your energy on jobs that barely pay, when Professor Hans himself is ready to support whatever you need.”

He could sense her frustration. Her words were true. Abhimanyu was no ordinary student—he possessed an exceptional memory and unprecedented innovative skill.

By the age of five, young Abhimanyu had built a solar-powered bicycle to ride to school, and that was just the beginning. The American League of Science had actively sought to recruit him before any formal contact could even be made.

“Your dad… he would have wanted you to take these opportunities and not lock your potential away,” Aadhya said gently.

“I just wanted some time. It was always better to learn more before planning where to work.”

“Learn? When corporations across the world are spending billions trying to build tech to counter these Astral crises and monsters, you’re out here helping people who won't even recognize you tomorrow! What was it you always say? ‘A shadow that helps guide light to the lost’?”

“Dad lived by that philosophy, Aadhya. He carried it until his very last moment. He was the man who taught me to put trust in people first. I’m just living by that principle. And once Didi gets married, I have to support the family. These corporations… they want to own me. But what about my mom? I can’t trade my family’s trust for billions of dollars.”

“I never asked you to,” she said softly, taking his arm. “I just want you to be treated with respect. You are a genius, and you deserve to live with the same trust and dignity you give to others. Mom agrees, you know. So for once… stop trying to be the guy who just gets pushed around. Do what makes you happy.”

“Okay, okay… I’ll think about it.”

***

Engrossed in conversation, they reached Abhimanyu’s home.

The apartment wasn’t lavish, but it comfortably housed a family of three: a simple kitchen and a shared room where the siblings rested after exhausting days. Half the time, Mrs. Devyani Rathore had to straighten out pillows and mediate the good-natured sibling rivalry between Abhi and Saloni. Yet neither child ever felt the absence of their late father, as their mother had poured every ounce of love into giving them everything she could.

The Rathore residence was located at 919 Vardhan Street, Central Mumbai—a pleasant, middle-class neighborhood.

“By the way,” Aadhya said, “I already told Mom I’m staying over tonight. So you can sleep on the sofa or in the kitchen. Saloni’s room is mine tonight.”

“Well, it isn’t my fault your birthday fell right before the work week,” she added. “And you wouldn’t leave a pretty girl walking home alone at midnight, would you? Besides, it won’t be long before I move in here and take over your house completely.”

“Aadhya, stop…” Abhimanyu’s cheeks burned despite the chilly weather. “Don’t embarrass me with talk about taking over my home.”

“Why? Would you rather have someone else invade your personal space?”

“No! Just… press the elevator button!”

Laughing, Abhi pressed the call button himself. Stepping inside the apartment, he kicked off his shoes and collapsed onto the plush sofa, exhausted. Aadhya immediately tugged at the hem of his shirt.

“Manners!”

“Seriously…?” he groaned, but dragged himself up to wash his hands anyway.

# The Next Morning

When Aadhya woke the next morning, she found Saloni making the bed while the whistle of a pressure cooker sounded from the kitchen.

“Didi… did he already leave?”

“Oh yes,” Saloni smiled. “You know Abhi. He always wants to rush out before anyone else even has a chance to wake up.”

“I thought he’d wait so we could go together…”

“Don’t worry, someday he will.”

“Stop teasing me! Did he take his lunch?”

“I hope so. Usually, I have to chase him down before heading to my office.”

“Why does he have to take everything so seriously, except for me?”

“That’s not true. If there’s anyone who matters to him more than all his equations, it’s you. He just has too much pride to say it out loud.”

From the kitchen, Mrs. Devyani Rathore called out:

“Girls, have some upma before you rush out! And dear, please make sure Abhi gets his lunchbox. I know it’s a hassle…”

“It’s no trouble at all, Mumma,” Aadhya smiled warmly.

“Saloni gets frustrated by his absentmindedness, but I worry about that boy’s restlessness. Just make sure he takes care of himself while he’s busy trying to fix the world.”

“I’ll make sure he eats, Mumma. Don’t worry.”

# The University of Excellence

The University of Excellence was situated close to the Mumbai coast. The warm sea breeze blended with the climate-control fields covering the campus. Students mingled in the courtyards—some testing experimental gadgets that occasionally singed the ancient stone facades, others debating Astral sciences and meta-species. This campus housed the nation’s brightest young minds, dedicated to advancing technology to protect the landmass they inhabited.

Inside the advanced physics laboratory, the hiss of cooling vents and the sharp whine of cutting tools filled the air.

Abhimanyu stood over his workbench, soldering delicate circuits into a spherical housing.

“Hello, Master Rathore. I hope you have an actual prototype today rather than just the equations you handed me last week.”

Abhimanyu pushed up his protective visor. “Hello, Professor Hans. As I theorized, the core needs neutronium for sufficient power, sir. The Earth’s gravitational field is too weak to generate the dilation curvature on its own. I tested uranium and plutonium isotopes, but the density simply doesn't budge.”

“You asked for neutronium, Master Rathore… But as you know, university funding is running tight. Nearly every grant is directed toward Astral research. The government has been desperate for another native Astral for the past fifteen years, and traditional physics is neglected. I would recommend you look into anti-Astral technologies instead. Perhaps your skills could help humanity find a defense against these pretentious saviors.”

“I must disagree, Professor,” Abhimanyu said firmly. “No country has ever successfully replicated Astral energy. It defies the standard model of physics. The deeper we dig into it, the more dangerous it becomes. Half the world relies on Astrals for defense… but if we can control spacetime gravitationally, we wouldn't need to depend on anyone.”

“And what if another calamity strikes?” Professor Hans asked. “Will human engineering be enough to stop monster incursions? I assume you heard about the recent devastation in Nairobi? The general population only survived because the Astrals intervened.”

“Mr. Rathore, pretending to defend people just to reap political glory is as monstrous as the calamities themselves,” Hans replied grimly. “Astrals come with an immense cost. Half of them would watch a city fall rather than let a rival Astral steal their prestige. It’s all politics, son. As scientists, our focus should be the unconditional advancement of mankind.”

Tavin’s words lingered in Abhimanyu’s thoughts long after the professor stepped away.

By afternoon, as the sun beamed down on the quad, Abhimanyu sat lost in contemplation.

“Are you daydreaming again?” Aadhya’s voice broke his trance.

“No, Aadhya… I was just thinking. I don’t believe all Astrals are bad. Nature always seeks balance. If monsters exist, perhaps nature chose Astrals to counter them.”

“Power breeds greed, dude,” Aadhya replied. “We were kids when Delhi was reduced to rubble by Shan Ziang, the so-called greatest Astral in the world. If someone at the top possesses such insatiable hunger for dominance, what can you expect from the rest? And now it costs ordinary citizens massive taxes just to fund defense shields we can barely afford.”

“It’s complicated, Aadhya… Vetramani stood against him.”

“Shh! Mentioning fallen Astrals attracts unwanted attention. That name has been practically forbidden for a decade. You don’t want a scene here on campus, do you?”

“Right… And now Professor Hans wants me to shift to anti-Astral weapons, when I’m so close to stabilizing the gravitational field at an artificial event horizon!”

“The same project that makes you forget your lunch?” She handed him the container.

“A gravitational field can dilate spacetime, Aadhya! If we can slow metabolic degradation safely, think of what it means for emergency medicine, cancer suppression, deep-space voyages…”

“Always thinking of saving the world,” she smiled affectionately.

A sudden smack landed on the back of Abhi’s head.

Before he could react, he was dragged toward the campus fountain, splashed with water as his friends laughed.

“Guys, c’mon! The laundry cycle costs twenty thousand Quids!” Abhi groaned, shaking off the water.

“Oh shut up, twig! Everything costs Quids with you. Enjoy life a little, Little Man!” laughed Hasan Miraj.

“Since when do Tavin’s prized pupils worry about Quids?” Kiraj Madhan chimed in.

“Give him a break,” Suraj smiled, stepping between them. “Abhimanyu, we’re heading to the Garden Villa tonight, and don’t you dare make excuses—we’re covering the bill upfront!”

“Are you insane, Suraj? Even if you pay, I have a shift at the restaurant tonight! I can’t just ditch work!”

“Oh c’mon, twig, don’t be a wuss! As if that place pays you enough to afford decent clothes anyway,” Hasan shot back.

“He has a point, Abhi,” Aadhya agreed. “You work non-stop. Take one evening off for your friends.”

“Fine, fine! Just tonight. No more blackmail for the rest of the year, deal?”

# The Garden Villa

That evening, Abhimanyu’s room was in complete disarray as he hunted for clean clothes.

“Moron! Will you stop making a mess the one time I want to relax?” Saloni shouted from the doorway, dodging a flying pair of socks.

“Didi, please, I need your help! I can’t find my tie!”

“Ugh… Check the yellow storage box under the wardrobe.”

She pulled out a sleek black tie.

“Here. Let’s see if Dad’s tie can make you look respectable for once.”

“I’ve never worn a tie before…”

“Hold still, or I’ll accidentally strangle you with it.”

As Abhimanyu looked into the mirror, straightened in the crisp dark suit, he caught a glimpse of his father’s posture in his own reflection.

“What is it?” Saloni asked softly.

“Nothing… I just felt Dad would have been proud seeing me wear this.”

Saloni’s eyes softened with emotion. “He would be. You look just like he did before his important presentations. You were his pride, Abhimanyu. But remember what he taught us: ‘Trust follows…’”

“‘…to those who learn to give trust to the souls around them,’” Abhimanyu finished in unison.

Saloni hugged him. “Go have fun. Don’t keep Aadhya waiting.”

Outside, jixonium street lamps illuminated the avenues as the sea breeze rolled in from the Arabian Sea.

When the cab pulled up to the Garden Villa, Abhimanyu caught his breath. Aadhya stood waiting near the entrance, looking radiant in a pastel pink skirt, silver heels, and a delicate obsidian-and-pearl necklace.

“Woah… You look like you stepped off a fashion runway,” Abhi said, stunned.

“If I was on a runway, Master Rathore, I wouldn’t be waiting around for a busybody like you,” she teased, blushing slightly. “Now, let’s get inside.”

The restaurant was magnificent—an open-air terrace overlooking the illuminated skyline, with soft ambient music and floating bioluminescent lights drifting overhead. Couples danced along the central floor while musicians played melodramatic ballads.

After an incredible dinner and laughter with their friends, the music shifted to a lively rhythm.

“C’mon, let’s dance!” Aadhya insisted, pulling Abhi toward the floor.

“I really don’t know how to dance…”

“Just move your body, twig!” Hasan called out from the table.

Abhi managed a few clumsy steps, making Aadhya laugh so hard she had to lean against him.

As the night wound down, the group stepped out into the cool evening air.

A faint whimpering sound echoed from a dark side street.

“Did you hear that?” Abhi stopped.

“Hear what?” Suraj asked.

“Wait here, I’m going to check.”

Abhi turned into a narrow alley. In the shadows under a flickering lamp, a group of intoxicated street thugs were kicking a terrified, cornered puppy.

“Hey! Leave the pup alone!” Abhimanyu shouted, his voice cutting through the alley.

A dozen men turned, their bloodshot eyes glaring at him.

“What do we have here? A hero?” the leader sneered, eyeing Aadhya who had followed behind. “And look at the pretty lady… Did you bring her for us?”

“Aadhya, get back,” Abhi said calmly.

“Abhi, no! They’re dangerous, let’s call the police—”

“Suraj, take her and leave. Now.”

The leader cracked his knuckles. “Nobody leaves our territory without permission, kid.”

“We’ll see about that, shithead.”

The burly thug lunged with a heavy swing. Half a second before the punch landed, Abhimanyu sidestepped, letting the fist graze his shoulder, and drove a vicious counter-punch into the man’s solar plexus. The thug collapsed, and Abhi shoved him into two others behind him, slamming them into the brick wall.

“Kill this brat!”

They charged two at a time, the narrow alley restricting their numbers. Athletic and fast, Abhimanyu ducked under a wild slash, drove his elbow into a man’s ribs, and delivered a spinning drop-kick that shattered another’s jaw.

The leader grabbed a rusty pipe from the rubble. “Enough! Back off, or I crush the dog’s skull!”

Abhi hesitated—and in that split second, two thugs swarmed him from behind. Boots and fists rained down on his ribs and jaw. A rib cracked; blood filled his mouth.

Gritting his teeth through the agony, Abhimanyu summoned his remaining strength. He grabbed the nearest assailant, drove his head into the man's nose, and hurled him into a row of metal dumpsters. The crash echoed like thunder. Spinning around, he delivered a devastating hook that sent the next thug sprawling across the pavement, spitting blood.

Seeing his ferocious resolve, the remaining thugs backed away in panic.

“Take the damn dog! Just let us go!” they scrambled, fleeing into the shadows.

Bleeding through his linen shirt, his tie stained red, Abhimanyu dropped to his knees. Breathing heavily, he gently extended a hand toward the trembling puppy.

“It’s okay, buddy… come here.”

He reached into his pocket and pulled out a packet of biscuits he had saved from the restaurant.

“Here… you must be hungry.”

The puppy cautiously took a step forward, licking his fingers before devouring the food. Abhi smiled through the pain, stroking its head.

“Abhi!” Suraj rushed into the alley. “Aadhya got the patrol guards, they’re rounding up the rest of those scum! Come on, we need to get you to Cortis Healthcare right now!”

“I’m fine…”

“Shut up and lean on me, you idiot,” Suraj commanded, supporting his weight as they walked out into the moonlight.

# Arabian Sea, near the Horn of Africa, 2204

High above the ocean, inside the darkened cargo hold of a military transport plane, a feminine voice whispered into the comms:

“Are the signals strong?”

“Affirmative, Sanghvi,” a voice responded through her earpiece. “Strong reasons to believe Shan could strike here. The Nagoya settlement coordinates point to this sector.”

“I hope this trip isn’t in vain…”

“Patience. Open the cargo hatch… I’m ready.”

The rear bay doors slid open into the howling gale. A lone figure leaped into the abyss, plummeting through the storm clouds.

As he pierced the cloud ceiling, brilliant golden light enveloped his body. His Astral Form erupted—a magnificent golden eagle with wings spanning the horizon.

The eagle circled, screeching across the turbulent waters as the titanic silhouette of Titanus Spewah breached the surface below.
